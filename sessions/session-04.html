CASE FILE 04 / 24 — NUVORI LYCEUM

Can Language Make a Country Feel Like Home?

Can learning a country's language create a genuine sense of belonging?

One position. One challenge. One conversation you'll have to hold live.

By the end of this session, you will be able to
  • I describe belonging, and its absence, in precise terms
  • II explain what fluency does and does not achieve
  • III distinguish integration from assimilation
  • IV discuss identity without reducing it to nationality
  • V speak with emotional honesty about a difficult situation
  • VI give personal advice and defend it kindly
Preparation

Watch, then reflect

Don't Ask Where I'm From, Ask Where I'm a Local
SPEAKER: TAIYE SELASI · PLATFORM: TED

The talk questions what we are really asking when we ask someone where they are from. The speaker proposes that we are shaped less by nationality than by the places where we hold our rituals, our relationships and our restrictions.

The Dilemma

The Guest with Very Good Manners

Ana left her home country at twenty-six. She is now forty.

For fourteen years she has lived in the same northern city, in the same apartment for the last nine. She holds citizenship. She works as a hospital pharmacist, a job that requires her to be precise in a language that was not her first.

Her language is, by any measure, excellent. Most colleagues say they no longer notice her accent. Twice this year, strangers have asked her for directions and then apologised for interrupting a local. She reads novels in it, argues in it, and sometimes dreams in it.

And yet, at her daughter's school, standing among the other parents at the winter concert, she describes herself privately as a guest with very good manners. She is never excluded. She is simply never quite inside. A joke lands and she understands every word and does not find it funny. Someone mentions a television programme everyone watched as children and she nods.

Her daughter Mira is twelve, born in the city, and belongs there completely. Mira understands her mother's first language and refuses to speak it. Last spring she asked Ana not to speak it in front of her friends. When Ana makes a rare error in the local language — a preposition, a small idiom — Mira corrects her, quickly and without unkindness, in the manner of someone correcting a child.

At home, however, Mira asks Ana to cook the dishes her grandmother makes, and keeps a small collection of voice messages from her grandmother on her phone.

Three weeks ago, Ana's mother, who is seventy-nine and lives alone, had a fall. She has recovered, but not entirely, and she will not move to a residential home. Ana's brother lives four hours away from her and travels constantly for work.

Ana knows that moving back would not simply mean returning home. After fourteen years away, she no longer speaks quite like the people she grew up with. Her brother occasionally jokes about expressions she has brought back from the northern language, and on her last visit an old friend told her, affectionately, "You sound foreign now."

Ana has been offered a post at a hospital twenty minutes from her mother's house. The salary is lower. The work is equivalent. The offer expires in six weeks.

Mira's father, from whom Ana separated amicably five years ago, lives in the same northern city and sees Mira every second weekend.

Ana has asked you — a close friend of eleven years, someone she trusts to be honest rather than kind — what you think she should do.

Your Initial Decision

Choose one position before class.

AStay. Fourteen years of rituals and relationships are here, and so is Mira's entire life.
BTake the post and move, bringing Mira with her.
CTake the post alone for a fixed period of one to two years; Mira stays with her father, with frequent visits.
DStay, and bring her mother to the northern city instead, whatever her mother currently says.
ERefuse the binary: reduce her hours, travel every month, and treat the situation as long-term management rather than a single decision.
FPropose a different solution.

Be prepared to explain your decision and consider an opposing position.

Language Toolkit

Useful Language

1. A sense of belonging — the feeling of being accepted as part of a place or group.Fourteen years of fluency have not given her a complete sense of belonging.
2. Feel at home — to feel comfortable, unobserved and unquestioned somewhere.She feels at home in the hospital, and rather less so at the school gate.
3. Put down roots — to settle somewhere and build a life there.She has put down roots without ever quite feeling native.
4. Adapt to a new culture — to change one's habits and expectations to fit a new environment.Adapting to a new culture is not the same as being absorbed by it.
5. Integrate into a community — to become a participating member of a group while remaining oneself.She integrated quickly at work and slowly everywhere else.
6. Feel like an outsider — to feel that one does not fully belong, whether or not others agree.You can hold the passport and still feel like an outsider.
7. Mother tongue — the language a person learned first and thinks in most naturally.Mira understands her mother's mother tongue but refuses to speak it.
8. Become fluent in — to reach the point of using a language easily and accurately.She became fluent years ago; belonging has proved much harder to measure.
9. Miss a cultural reference — to fail to recognise something everyone else grew up with.She understands every word of the joke and still misses the reference.
10. Blend in — to become indistinguishable from the people around you.She blends in perfectly until someone mentions a childhood programme.
11. Straddle two cultures — to live between two cultures, belonging partly to each.Mira straddles two cultures but has chosen one of them.
12. Uproot someone — to remove someone from the place where their life is established.Taking the post would mean uprooting a twelve-year-old from the only city she knows.

Acknowledging Complexity

There is no version of this decision in which nobody loses something.
Whatever she chooses, she will be choosing between two real duties.
The question is not which option is right, but which loss she can live with.

Expressing Caution

We should be careful not to decide her life according to our own values.
A twelve-year-old's objection is not decisive, but it is evidence.
Six weeks is a deadline, not a reason.

Important Distinctions

Fluency and Belonging — Fluency is command of a language. Belonging is the sense of being at ease among people who assume you are one of them. Fluency can be measured; belonging can only be reported.
Integration and Assimilation — Integration means participating fully in a community while retaining visible elements of one's previous identity. Assimilation involves adopting the dominant culture to the point that those differences become less visible. The boundary between the two is not always clear, and people disagree about whether assimilation represents loss, choice or adaptation.
Homesickness and Nostalgia — Homesickness is longing for a place that still exists. Nostalgia is longing for a time that does not. The first can be answered by a plane ticket; the second cannot.
Accent and Proficiency — An accent indicates where a speaker has been. Proficiency indicates what a speaker can do. People routinely mistake the first for evidence about the second.
Being Accepted and Feeling Accepted — Others may fully accept a person who does not feel accepted. The gap between the two is where much of this case lives.
Your Country and Your Home — A country is a legal and political fact. A home is an accumulation of habits, faces and streets. They frequently coincide, and they are not the same thing.

Grammar in Use

She used to translate in her head. She is used to the winters now. She never got used to the silence at the school gate.

Discussion Map

What we'll explore

What Ana actually lacks, given how much she has · whether fluency was ever likely to produce belonging · what Mira's refusal to speak her mother's language means · the difference between being welcomed and being at home · whose belonging counts most in the decision · what Ana owes her mother, her daughter and herself · whether "home" for Ana is now a place or a period of her life · how far a friend should go when asked for an honest opinion.

The Locality Lens

  • The Rituals Which daily habits and routines anchor Ana's life, and where do they happen?
  • The Relationships Where are the people whose absence would change her days?
  • The Restrictions What limits her freedom to be local somewhere: work, money, custody, the passport, the language?
  • The Language Is language a fourth test of locality, or the medium through which the other three are built?
  • The Second Belonging Mira belongs completely where she is. How much weight should a twelve-year-old's belonging carry against her mother's?
  • The Verdict Which of these should decide the case, and what would you actually say to Ana?
