The Cost of Avoiding Conflict

Does avoiding conflict protect relationships, or does it usually make problems worse?

One position. One challenge. One recommendation you'll have to defend live.

By the end of this session, you will be able to
  • I describe how unresolved tension develops
  • II explain why people avoid difficult conversations
  • III distinguish productive disagreement from destructive conflict
  • IV separate facts from assumptions
  • V recommend a constructive intervention
  • VI open and structure a difficult conversation
  • VII defend and reconsider a position
Preparation

Watch, then reflect

The Gift of Conflict
SPEAKER: AMY E. GALLO · PLATFORM: TED

The talk examines why people often avoid disagreement, how silence can damage relationships and organisations, and how conflict can become productive when it is approached with calm, confidence and compassion.

Preparation Questions

  1. Main Idea — What does the speaker want us to reconsider about conflict?
  2. Memorable Point — Which claim, example or practical suggestion stayed with you?
  3. Your Reaction — Which ideas did you find convincing or difficult to accept?
  4. Possible Limitation — Are there situations in which speaking up may create more harm than benefit?
  5. Professional Connection — Can you connect the talk to a situation you have observed, at work or elsewhere? You do not need to mention names or confidential details.
  6. Conditions for Success — What needs to be present for disagreement to become productive?
The Dilemma

The Partnership That Stopped Talking

Nadia and Tomas founded a twelve-person design studio nine years ago. Nadia manages clients and finances. Tomas leads the creative team. For most of the company's history, the partnership worked well, and each trusted the other's area of responsibility.

For the past two years, however, Nadia has privately disagreed with one of Tomas's habits: he accepts late client changes without charging for the extra work. In Nadia's view, this overloads the team, puts deadlines at risk and quietly damages the company's finances.

She has never raised the issue directly. She tells herself that Tomas takes criticism personally, that the moment is never right and that an argument might damage their partnership. Instead, she has become colder in meetings, made sarcastic remarks and increasingly shifted important decisions to email.

Tomas has noticed the distance but interprets it differently. He believes Nadia has become overly controlling and does not understand the importance of protecting client relationships. He sees flexibility as one of the studio's strengths and assumes that the financial pressure is manageable.

The disagreement has not remained private. Last month, a senior designer resigned. In her exit conversation, she described the atmosphere as "polite but poisonous" and said that the whole team could feel the tension between the founders.

Then the situation escalated. A major project ran three weeks late, and Tomas discovered that Nadia had quietly asked the client to contact her directly, bypassing him. Tomas felt betrayed. Nadia insists that she was protecting the project. The two founders had their first open argument in nine years, in front of the team, and have barely spoken since.

They have now asked a business mentor — you — to advise them on what to do next.

Your Initial Decision

Choose one position before class.

ARecommend a structured private conversation between the two partners, carefully prepared but without a third party.
BBring in a professional mediator to guide a series of facilitated conversations.
CBegin with separate meetings to establish the facts, then decide whether a joint conversation is appropriate.
DIntroduce a structural solution: clearly divided responsibilities, a written decision-making protocol and a review system.
EAdvise them to begin planning a managed separation because the partnership may no longer be repairable.
FPropose a different solution.

Be prepared to explain your decision and consider an opposing position.

Language Toolkit

Useful Language

1. Avoid a difficult conversation — to delay or refuse a discussion because it may be uncomfortable.Avoiding a difficult conversation may provide temporary relief, but the underlying issue remains.
2. Underlying tension — hidden or unspoken strain in a relationship.The resignation revealed underlying tension that the whole team had already noticed.
3. Let resentment build — to allow anger or bitterness to grow over time.Nadia let resentment build instead of explaining what she needed.
4. Bring an issue into the open — to discuss a problem honestly rather than leave it hidden.The partners now need to bring the disagreement into the open.
5. Address the root cause — to deal with the main reason for a problem rather than only its visible effects.A new reporting system may help, but it will not address the root cause on its own.
6. Clear the air — to discuss a disagreement so that tension and misunderstanding can be reduced.A well-structured conversation could help them clear the air.
7. Escalate the conflict — to make a disagreement more serious or difficult to resolve.Sarcasm and indirect decisions gradually escalated the conflict.
8. Rebuild trust — to gradually restore confidence between people after it has been damaged.Any agreement will only survive if both partners are willing to rebuild trust.
9. Make assumptions about someone's intentions — to form a judgement without sufficient evidence.Both partners have made assumptions about each other's intentions.
10. A neutral third party — someone with no personal interest in the conflict who helps both sides communicate.A neutral third party may be necessary if trust has deteriorated too far.
11. Seek common ground — to identify shared needs, interests or goals.Their strongest common ground is the future of the studio and the well-being of the team.
12. Reach a workable agreement — to find a solution that may not be perfect but can function in practice.The immediate goal is to reach a workable agreement on clients, deadlines and decision-making.

Important Distinctions

Avoidance and Strategic Delay — Avoidance means postponing an issue mainly because it feels uncomfortable. Strategic delay means choosing a later moment for a clear reason while still committing to address the issue.
Productive Conflict and Personal Attack — Productive conflict focuses on evidence, needs, decisions and possible solutions. A personal attack questions someone's character, intelligence or motives.
Diplomacy and Evasion — A diplomatic person tells the truth carefully. An evasive person avoids telling the truth at all.
Intention and Impact — A person may not intend to create harm, but their behaviour may still have a damaging effect.
Mediation and Arbitration — A mediator helps both sides reach their own agreement. An arbitrator listens to both sides and then makes a decision.
A Truce and a Resolution — A truce pauses open conflict without settling its cause. A resolution addresses the cause so the same conflict is less likely to return.

Discussion Map

What we'll explore

The original source of the disagreement · why Nadia remained silent · how Tomas interpreted the situation · the difference between facts and assumptions · the effects of sarcasm, indirect communication and client bypassing · the impact on the wider team · whether the conflict can be repaired · whether conversation, mediation, structural change or separation is most realistic · what evidence would show that trust is being rebuilt.

The Conflict-Escalation Lens

  • The Source What was the original disagreement actually about?
  • The Silence What was avoided, by whom and for what reasons?
  • The Stories What explanation does each partner give for the other's behaviour?
  • The Escalation Which actions, assumptions or silences made the conflict worse?
  • The Turning Point Which event made the conflict impossible to ignore?
  • The Conversation Needed What now needs to be said, heard and clarified?
  • The Working Agreement What practical arrangement could be tested, and what safeguards would keep it constructive?
  • The Repair or Separation Test What would show that the partnership should continue, and what would show that it should end?
